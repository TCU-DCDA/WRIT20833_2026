Day 1 · the through-line

Connotations & Code

Why no number is ever neutral

The opening lecture left us with a claim: data doesn't arrive on its own. Getting from what happens in people's lives to an "insight" about them takes many steps — deciding what to record, how to count it, and what to call it — and someone makes every one of those decisions. This lecture turns that claim on the very first thing you'll touch — a plain number — and asks what it can, and can't, tell us on its own.

Here is a fact about the corpus we'll work with all term: 93 YouTube comments on a Texas law that puts the Ten Commandments in public-school classrooms. (A corpus is simply a collection of texts gathered for analysis.) The comments sit under one CBS News video from August 20, 2025, reporting that a federal judge had blocked the law in some school districts, and they were copied within the following month using a browser scraping tool. So many of them are reacting to the judge as much as to the law. The tool caught top-level comments but not the replies under them, and blank and duplicate entries were removed. Some comments are tiny: one reads, in full, "Of Texas." And one more choice shapes everything you'll compute: a few long comments ran over several lines, and the cleaning split them at their line breaks. So the file you'll load has 123 rows, not 93, and some rows are only pieces of a comment. The export kept no names or dates, either, so we don't know how many people wrote these comments, or exactly when. They are not a sample of Texans, or even of YouTube: they're whoever chose to comment on that one video, and whatever the tool caught. We'll come back to who that leaves out.

Close reading, distant reading

Why bring code into a humanities class at all? Here's one answer. Close reading is what you already do with a single text: slow, careful attention to what it says and what it implies. Distant reading uses code to look for patterns across more text than any one person could read. Code gives us distance; the humanities tell us what we're looking at. Ninety-three comments is few enough to read closely, which makes this corpus a good place to practice both, and to see that distant reading still needs a close reader.

What does the count say?

In those 93 comments, ignoring capitals, the word "commandments" appears 25 times. "Constitution" appears 8.

A flat, true, countable fact. It's tempting to read it at once — the crowd cares more about scripture than law. But ask what else could have produced it. The video is about the Ten Commandments, so the word was always going to dominate. The count itself rests on a choice: to a computer, Constitution and constitution are two different words, and our file has four of each. Ignore capitals and they merge into 8; keep them and each counts only 4. Someone decided which before the number existed, and that is the general lesson: the methods used to clean and prepare data can literally change the outcome. And the comments come only from people who chose to post. Before we run a single line of code, ask the humanist's question: what could this number mean, what else could explain it, and who decided what got counted?

Denotation and connotation

You already own the tool this lecture needs — you've used it on words your whole life.

  • A word's denotation is what it literally points to. House, home, and residence denote the same thing: a building people live in.
  • Its connotation is what it implies beyond that: the emotional resonance and the particular point of view it brings along — warmth, money, bureaucracy, belonging. Home and residence denote alike and connote worlds apart.

By close reading, we can determine a text's tone, attitude, and mood — not just what it says, but what it implies. Later in the term, we'll look at ways a computer can try to detect what's implied.

Now the move that makes you a data humanist: numbers have connotation too. "Single-mother household" denotes a category on a form; it connotes a whole contested story about blame and worth, and which story you hear depends on who's speaking. The denotation is the surface. The connotation is where the culture lives — and what we build the argument from.

The myth of neutral data

Three comforting things people say about data. Each one is too simple.

  • "Data doesn't lie." — It doesn't have to lie to mislead. It can simply leave things out.
  • "The numbers speak for themselves." — Numbers never speak for themselves. Someone always speaks for them, by choosing the frame.
  • "It's just objective analysis." — Every dataset is a record of somebody's choices about what was worth counting, and what wasn't.

Data carries the values, assumptions, and blind spots of whoever collected, sorted, and counted it. That isn't a flaw you can scrub out. It's the opening lecture's diagram again: every arrow there was a choice, and so is every column here. A dataset is a record of choices before it is a record of anything else.

Same numbers, three stories

Two plain numbers:

School A — average SAT 1240. School B — average SAT 980.

Watch the same denotation spin into three different connotations, each one quietly handing the blame to someone else:

  • Deficit story — School B's students are underperforming. (It's the kids.)
  • Systemic story — School B is under-resourced and underfunded. (It's the system.)
  • Cultural story — the SAT measures one particular kind of knowledge. (It's the test.)

Nothing in the numbers tells you which story is true. They show a difference, not its cause; to argue about causes, you'd need more evidence than the two scores. Even the difference can be staged: draw the same two bars with the axis starting at 900 instead of 0, and School B seems to collapse. The numbers are the easy part. Choosing the story is the human part — and it is already an argument about fairness, wearing the costume of arithmetic.

Who's doing the telling?

If numbers don't tell their own story, someone does — usually someone with power over the frame.

  • Institutions define the categories: a government writes the census boxes, a university decides what "success" counts as, a platform decides what "engagement" means.
  • Media sets the frame: the headline, the axis a chart starts at, the context that gets cut for space.
  • Researchers choose the question: what gets studied, who gets sampled, and who paid for the asking.

So the reflex this course wants to build: every time a number lands in front of you, ask who collected this, why, and whose story does it tell?

The missing and the uncounted

The loudest thing about a dataset is often what isn't in it.

Our 93 comments are not "the public." They're the slice of the public that posted — people online, fired up enough to type, willing to be seen arguing. The woman from the opening lecture made the choice to post her thoughts about the debate. Her neighbor felt just as strongly but never typed a word, and is not captured by the dataset at all. Both women had the wifi and the time; plenty of people don't, and they never had a chance to be counted.

You've heard this move before. Politicians of every stripe make it: "The American people want…" or "The good people of Texas feel…" Each claims to speak for everyone on the strength of whoever wrote in, called, or showed up. Write "Texans think…" from these 93 comments, and you've made the same move. What the data supports is narrower and more honest: these commenters wrote…

Absence from the data is not absence from reality. Some of the most important voices in any cultural question are exactly the ones the counting never reaches. A good distant reading stays honest about the silence at its edges.

So: code is not neutral

Code inherits every connotation above, and then does two things more. First, it amplifies them. A human reader might pause over an odd comment and ask whether this case is different; a program applies the same rule to every row, thousands of times, without hesitating. Whatever assumption the rule carries gets carried out at scale. Second, code adds its own connotations, because code runs on logical patterns that someone programmed — if this, then that — and writing each pattern is a decision.

Over the next eight weeks you will be the one deciding. What counts as a "word" when you split a comment? Which words are "stopwords" worth deleting? What score makes a comment "positive"? Where do you cut one topic from the next? Each is a small act of interpretation that looks like a neutral function.

In code, those decisions are concrete operations. You'll split text into words, count them, delete the ones on a stopword list (common words like the and of that an analyst chooses to skip), score each comment and set a threshold (a cutoff that turns a continuous score into a category like "positive"), and group comments that use similar words. Every one of those verbs hides a choice.

Take one rule you'll find in countless tutorials: drop every word used fewer than five times. Why would anyone do that? The motives are sensible. Rare words are often noise — typos and scraps like chruch, obay, and govmt. A word that appears once can't show a pattern; it can't rise or fall over time or turn up beside other words, and counting methods run on repetition. And rare words are most of any vocabulary: in our file, 457 of the 749 different words appear exactly once. In a corpus of millions of words they slow later methods like topic modeling and crowd every chart, so many tools make the cut a default step.

But look at what it costs. In our file, that rule deletes 651 of the 749 different words — slavery, adultery, empathy, Muslim, and Jewish among them — while keeping the, to, and of. Even separation, as in church and state, falls below the line: it appears four times, plus once spelled "seperation," so a single typo decides whether it survives. And you'll make a cousin of this cut yourself, every time you ask for the most_common(10) words.

So those choices don't merely encode a value — they argue one. That makes them rhetoric: the art of persuasion, or how a speaker or writer shapes a message to move a particular audience — what to say, in what order, and what to leave out. Aristotle defined it as the ability to see, in any given case, "the available means of persuasion." A frequency cut does some of that leaving-out on your behalf. Hand a reader the surviving words and they'll conclude the conversation was about commandments, religion, and God, never learning that anyone raised slavery or empathy at all. Which number you make the headline, which chart you reach for, what you put first and what you bury: every one is a claim about what matters, aimed at whoever reads your result. Code isn't only structure; it's rhetoric. You'll feel that fully when you go public with an analysis at the end of the term — but it starts here, in the smallest function.

That's the trap and the responsibility at once: data and software look objective precisely because they hide from the general user the choices that shaped them. Your job as a humanist who codes is a double move: read data and code like a text, for denotation and connotation, and write code that shows your choices. Leave the #comment that says what you chose, and why. Your #comments are not housekeeping. They're where you take the opportunity to be honest and transparent about your choices.

Someone always cleans the text

The books you read for class didn't arrive that way. Before you saw a word, editors and publishers standardized the spelling and punctuation, fixed the typos, and chose which version, which edition, and which words to keep. You've been reading cleaned data your whole life. You just never saw the cleaning.

You clean as you read

Comments and posts written on the fly don't get an editor, so you become one without noticing. One comment in our corpus begins "Seperation of chruch and state." You read separation of church and state and move on; you don't stop to wonder what govmt or obay means. A computer does none of that. To Python, seperation and separation are two different words, as different as cat and dog. That's why cleaning text in code is worth slowing down for: it makes your invisible reading habits visible.

You clean as you listen, too

You do the same thing when you listen. Someone says um every third word, picks the wrong word, mispronounces a name, and you hear what they meant. Speech-to-text software has to make those decisions explicitly: which ums to keep, which slips to fix. Every transcript is a cleaned version of what was said.

Today, you're the editor

In the code-along you'll try three string methods, and each one is a choice. .lower() decides whether Constitution and constitution count as one word. .split() decides what counts as a "word" at all — what about don't, or church-and-state, or state. with its period still attached? .replace() lets you fix seperation, which raises the question of who decides what's a typo. Run two real comments through lowercasing, splitting, and counting, and three ideas come out as six different words. Every cleaning step is an edit. Comment why you made it.

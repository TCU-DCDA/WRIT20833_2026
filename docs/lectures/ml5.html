Day 4 · the through-line

Collective Memory

What a culture keeps, and what it lets go

Today you learn lists and loops — how to hold many things at once, and how to do the same thing to every one of them. That's the technical skill. The human story underneath it is memory: who gets remembered, who gets forgotten, and what changes when the record is kept by a machine that keeps whatever it is given.

violations = []
for person in community:
    if person["broke_a_rule"]:
        violations.append(person)

A list, a loop, a condition — and a community that now keeps a permanent record of everyone who ever broke one of its rules. Let's talk about what that does.

Two kinds of memory

For most of human history, a community's memory was a human thing — and being human, it forgot.

  • People forget, a little at a time. Details fade. Stories soften with each retelling. Someone gets a second chance, and the village eventually lets a thing go.
  • A database keeps the row exactly as it was written. A record doesn't mellow with age. Ten years on it reads the same, and it can still turn up when you apply for a job, an apartment, or a loan.

That shift — from a memory that forgets to one that can't — is what this lecture is about. Forgetting was never only a failure. It is also how people get a second chance.

A list is a population

A list looks like the most harmless thing in Python:

community = ["Mary", "John", "Kate", "Ahmed", "Rosa", ...]

But notice what just happened. A moment ago these were people. Now they're a population — a thing you can sort, filter, slice, and count all at once.

This week you'll watch that happen to your own writing. At the end of class today you'll each write a short post in a role-play discussion, and on Wednesday those posts become a Python list: names removed, persona kept, one post per item. Whatever the record doesn't have a place for — who wrote it, what they meant by it, what they'd say if asked again — it doesn't keep.

The list treats every item the same. That move is enormously powerful (it's how you'll hear a hundred voices at once) and quietly dangerous (it's how a crowd of people becomes a single number someone can act on).

A loop is a verdict, repeated

A village elder judged case by case — knowing the person, weighing the circumstances, leaving room for the exception, the second look. A loop doesn't.

for person in population:
    apply_the_same_rule(person)

A for loop applies one rule to every item in a list, the same way, thousands of times a second. A loop makes no mistakes in the usual sense: it does exactly what the rule says, to every item. "The same rule for everyone" sounds like the definition of fairness, and in one way it is: nobody gets special treatment. It also means nobody gets a second look. A loop can't make an exception unless someone wrote the exception in first. Remember the 650 loan cutoff from Classification Logic? A loop is how that one line reaches every applicant in the file.

The power to count

Counting feels neutral — you're just tallying what's there. But someone has to choose the categories first, and choosing categories is choosing what's worth seeing.

Counter(violations).most_common()

One line, and a messy human record becomes a ranked list of "what's wrong here." A Counter reports only the categories it was given. Anything that fits none of them doesn't appear in its output at all.

So ask: who chose the categories? Who gets to see the tally? When does a count become a reason — for a policy, a denial, a crackdown? And the question from Connotations & Code that never goes away: what happened to the people who fit no category, and so were never counted at all?

Historical echoes

None of this is new. Only the machinery is.

The Bellevue Almshouse, opened in 1816 on Manhattan's East River, was New York City's hospital, poorhouse, and asylum in one. People who could pay went to private hospitals; Bellevue was where the city sent everyone else: the sick poor, orphans, people judged insane, victims of contagious disease. During the Irish Famine of 1845–52, over a million Irish emigrants landed in New York. Many who fell ill or ran out of money after arriving ended up at Bellevue, and a clerk wrote each one into an admissions ledger. (In May 1847 the city and state created a Commission of Emigration with its own hospitals, and Irish admissions to Bellevue fell sharply.)

The historian Anelise Shrout describes what records like these were for:

Nineteenth-century immigration data was produced with the express purpose of reducing people to
bodies; bodies to easily quantifiable aspects; and assigning value to those aspects which proved that the
marginalized people to [whom] they belonged were worth less than their elite counterparts.

— Anelise Hanson Shrout, "(Re)Humanizing Data: Digitally Navigating the Bellevue Almshouse", Current Research in Digital History 1 (2018)

Those ledgers were transcribed in 2004–06 for NYU's Digital Almshouse Project, and Shrout cleaned the transcription and published it as data: 12,110 admissions of Irish-born people between 1845 and 1847. Before counting anything, read some rows. Here are the file's first eight, showing five of its eleven columns:

date_in     age  gender  occupation  disease
1847-05-31   54  m       laborer     destitution
1847-05-19   91  m       laborer     destitution
1847-06-02   25  m       NA          recent emigrant
1847-03-03   51  m       laborer     recent emigrant
1847-05-22   28  m       laborer     recent emigrant
1847-05-27   35  m       laborer     lame
1847-05-27   63  m       laborer     recent emigrant
1847-05-27   40  m       laborer     destitution

"disease" is the heading the ledger itself used (the file calls its cleaned version reason_cleaned), and NA means nothing was recorded. You can read all 12,110 rows. Here is the first one as a Python dictionary:

{"date_in": "1847-05-31", "age": 54, "occupation": "laborer", "disease": "destitution"}

Run a loop and a Counter over the "disease" entry in every row, and after "sickness" the two most common entries are recent emigrant (3,147) and destitution (1,616). Being poor, and being an immigrant on public aid, were entered under "disease." ("Recent emigrant" did not measure time in the city: the project read it as marking people bonded to shipping firms who were now relying on the city's aid; see the archived Digital Almshouse Project. Its counts come from an earlier version of the data, so they differ from the ones here.)

The tools changed. The power to reduce, sort, and leave people out did not. When you write your own loops this week, you're picking up a very old instrument.

What we choose to keep

So lists and loops were never just technical skills. They're how a culture decides what it keeps and what it lets go — and now you're one of the people writing them.

At the end of class today you'll write the first entries in a small class record. On Wednesday we'll look at what survived the trip into data, and what didn't. Here, and every time you count and sort people's words this term, keep the live question open: am I remembering these people, or just reducing them? And the one the whole course keeps asking — make the choice visible. What you keep, what you drop, and what you let stay forgotten is an argument; leave the #comment that admits it.

Going deeper (optional): Paul Connerton, How Societies Remember; Viktor Mayer-Schönberger, Delete: The Virtue of Forgetting in the Digital Age; José van Dijck, Mediated Memories in the Digital Age.

Your turn: a class record

At the end of class you'll start a small record of your own. The proposal, made up for this activity: the library will convert one of its quiet study floors into a collaborative workspace (group tables, whiteboards, talking allowed), open the same hours as the rest of the building. Each of you gets a persona card. Writing as that person, you'll post a short response in the D2L discussion The Quiet Floor during class, and reply to a different persona by Tuesday at 11:59 p.m. The full instructions are in the D2L topic.

Write it yourself, without AI, and take the person on your card seriously: their circumstances, not a stereotype of them. On Wednesday these posts become our practice data. Like Bellevue's clerks, we'll choose the columns: the post, the persona, and what it replied to. Your name won't be one of them.

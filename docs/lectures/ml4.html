Day 9 · the through-line

AI Agency

Borrow the tool — don't outsource the thinking

You can now turn to an AI and say "write me ten lines of Python to score the sentiment of these comments," and ten seconds later you have ten working lines. The question this course cares about isn't can the machine write the code — it plainly can. It's the one you'll have to answer about every line it hands you: do you understand it well enough to judge it?

Borrow freely; read what you borrow

AI-assisted coding genuinely lowers the barrier, and borrowing isn't new: programmers have always borrowed from manuals, colleagues, and Stack Overflow, and you'll lean on VADER, pandas, and Gensim all term. The danger is borrowing what you can't read. Code you can't explain is a black box: you can't say whether it will hold on real data, or fix it when it breaks. The quieter failure is pseudo-agency: feeling powerful ("I built this!") while growing more dependent, unable to vouch for the thing with your name on it.

Scaffold, not replacement

So the line isn't "AI or no AI." It's scaffold vs. replacement: who's responsible for what. As a scaffold, the AI writes boilerplate while you're responsible for the question, the definitions, the interpretation, and every line. As a replacement, you paste the answer and submit, and the AI has spoken for you. Hence the syllabus rule: explain any code you submit, and note in a #comment where AI helped and what you changed.

Code is borrowed; your voice isn't

Learning a language means studying what each piece does and determining through trial and error how to adapt and fix it. Output you can't explain is ventriloquism: the words come out of your mouth, but someone else chose them. Here the rule flips. Nobody's voice lives in a stopword list: borrow (and credit) the code you need. But your reflections, posts, and capstone essay are your voice, forged in the friction of finding your own words. Let an AI write your code and you've saved time; let it write your prose and you've handed away the one thing this course exists to grow. Borrow the tool; never outsource the voice. (Ted Chiang calls ChatGPT "a blurry JPEG of the web." A voice worth having is the opposite of a blur.)

Eloquence is not evidence

The machine's writing is fluent whether it is right or wrong. Polished prose used to be expensive, so polish was a rough proxy for the work behind it. Language models broke that link: fluency is free, and an invented claim reads exactly like a checked one. Our instincts haven't caught up; a well-made paragraph still feels vetted. Stephen Colbert's word for that feeling is truthiness: believing a claim is true because it feels true, without regard to evidence, logic, or facts. It can range from ignorant assertions of falsehoods to deliberate propaganda meant to sway opinion.

Not lying. Something else.

Philosophy has a blunter word. Harry Frankfurt distinguished the liar, who knows the truth and hides it, from the bullshitter, who doesn't care what's true, only what lands. Hicks, Humphries and Slater (2024) argue that "hallucination" is the wrong name for AI errors: a model produces plausible next words, and when they're true, that's incidental. You needn't accept their label to take the point: fluency can't count as evidence.

An old problem, with a Greek name

None of this is new. In Plato's Gorgias, Gorgias boasts that a skilled speaker would beat a physician in a debate about medicine, before an audience that knew no medicine (Gorgias 456b–c, 459a–b), because eloquence feels like expertise from the inside. Plato calls the conflict between rational judgment and our pleasures and pains a profound amathia, or ignorance (Laws 689a–b). For this course, "un-wisdom" names a related danger: having information and assistance without exercising judgment. It's the last arrow on the Day 1 diagram, failed: knowledge that never becomes wisdom, because the labor between them got outsourced.

The fix is friction

Two things follow. Amathia points at us, never at them: it isn't a diagnosis for the commenters collected in our dataset or anyone across a political argument; it's the temptation you face when an eloquent answer arrives faster than your judgment. And the fix isn't an attitude ("trust AI less"), which won't beat a gut habit. It's procedural friction: run the code, check the citation, find the source, read it line by line. Keep it next to the score is never the meaning: eloquence is not evidence.

Today: read the code

That's today's lab: two pieces of AI-written code to read line by line, test on our comments, and improve. Both run without an error. Both are wrong. Here's the kind of word counter an AI will write for you:

def count_words(text):
    counts = {}
    for word in text.split():
        counts[word] = counts.get(word, 0) + 1
    return counts

It runs and looks finished. But what is word on each pass? What does .get(word, 0) do? (You hand-built this function in the Dictionaries & Functions code-along, with an if/else where the .get is.) And on our 93 comments, .split() keeps capitals and punctuation, so "commandments" comes back as five keys: ask for counts["commandments"] and you get 13, where a reader sees the word 25 times.

What understanding looks like

You understand code when you can say what each line takes in and hands back, name the type of each important variable, predict what an unusual input will do, name one thing it gets wrong, and change one thing and test that it worked. Carry one question out of here: are you learning to think computationally, or outsourcing the thinking? The same tool allows either. As always: make the choice visible: cite the AI, and say what you changed.

Going deeper (optional): Douglas Rushkoff, Program or Be Programmed; Ted Chiang, "ChatGPT Is a Blurry JPEG of the Web" (The New Yorker, 2023); Nick Montfort, Exploratory Programming for the Arts and Humanities; Harry Frankfurt, On Bullshit (2005); Michael Townsen Hicks, James Humphries & Joe Slater, "ChatGPT Is Bullshit" (Ethics and Information Technology, 2024); Plato, Gorgias (the original argument about persuasion without knowledge).
